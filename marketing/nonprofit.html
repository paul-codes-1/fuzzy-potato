What advocacy teams actually do with CivicLens

Less abstract pitch, more concrete Tuesdays. Here is what organizers, policy staff, and volunteer research teams use it for on an average week.

Track council members on your issue

Pull every vote and every statement from a specific councilmember on housing, climate, or whatever issue you work on. Build a receipt, not a rumor.

Alert on agenda keywords

Set a watch for “affordable housing” or “tree canopy” or any phrase. CivicLens emails you the moment it appears on an upcoming agenda — before the meeting, not after.

Build a shareable vote record

Export structured vote data for voter guides, endorsement decisions, and member mailings. Every row links back to the exact minute in the meeting video.

Find every mention of a developer or contractor

Search the full archive for a company name, a land parcel, a grant program, or an LLC. Surface prior discussions, contracts, and conflicts you would have missed.

Generate member digests

Weekly or post-meeting digests your members will actually read: what passed, who voted, which items got pushback in public comment, and what is coming up next week.

Pull quotes & timestamps for op-eds

Ask a question, get answers with direct quotes, speaker attribution, and clickable [MM:SS] timestamps. Ready for testimony prep, a press release, or a social post that holds up.

A Tuesday before CivicLens. A Tuesday after.

Most advocacy research still looks like this: someone loads the Granicus player and takes notes. We think the time of the person doing that is worth more than that.

Before

The staffer at 9pm on Tuesday

  • • Three-hour council meeting is somewhere in a 3.5-hour video. Rezoning item is item 14 of 22. You fast-forward, miss it, scrub back.
  • • You take notes in a Google Doc. Which councilmember was that? Rewind. Listen again. Type out the quote by hand.
  • • The motion happened, but there was no roll call, just “any opposed? seeing none, the motion carries.” Who voted? Unclear.
  • • Next week there is another meeting. You never get back to last week's. The thread is lost. The alert you meant to send never goes out.
After

The staffer at 9am on Wednesday

  • ✓ Digest is in the inbox. What passed, what failed, who voted which way, and which items got pushback in public comment — all in under 5 minutes of reading.
  • ✓ A keyword alert fired last night: the contested rezoning hit the agenda for next Tuesday. You already have a draft action alert going out this afternoon.
  • ✓ You click a timestamp link. The video jumps straight to 1:42:18, the exact moment the councilmember said the quote you want for the op-ed.
  • ✓ You spend your afternoon on strategy, not on scrubbing a video player. The archive remembers so you don't have to.

Three ways teams actually use it

Composite scenarios based on how advocacy organizations use meeting intelligence tools in practice. Outcomes described qualitatively — we don't quote hockey-stick numbers we cannot back up.

Housing coalition

Tracking rezoning votes across a metro

Persona: Two-person policy team at a tenants' rights coalition covering a mid-size city council plus four suburb councils.

Pain: Rezoning hearings happen on different nights in different cities. They can physically attend maybe one per week. Everything else they hear about from members after the fact, if at all.

How they use it: Keyword alerts on “rezoning,” “density bonus,” and “inclusionary.” Saved searches on each city. A weekly digest they forward to their members list.

Outcome: Faster turnaround on action alerts. Fewer meetings missed. More time writing testimony instead of transcribing.

Environmental group

Monitoring EPA compliance mentions

Persona: A small watershed advocacy org with one full-time staffer and a board of volunteer scientists.

Pain: Stormwater, consent decrees, and permitting show up in council and utility board meetings without warning. By the time it's in the newspaper, the decision is made.

How they use it: Tracked terms: “MS4,” “consent decree,” specific creek names, and the names of the treatment plants they monitor. Ask-the-archive for historical context before every public comment.

Outcome: Shows up prepared more often. Public comments cite prior council statements by minute and second. Board packs get written faster.

Police accountability

Searchable archive of oversight meetings

Persona: A volunteer-led community oversight coalition that attends civilian review board, police commission, and budget hearings.

Pain: Oversight meetings are long, technical, and often scheduled mid-day. Volunteers cannot attend every one. No usable public index exists of what was said, by whom, when.

How they use it: Ingested two years of archived oversight meetings. Share a public-facing searchable page with their membership. Pull timestamped quotes for annual reports.

Outcome: Members can self-serve research. Annual report took weeks instead of months to draft. New volunteers ramp up without having to watch 60 hours of video.

These are composite scenarios drawn from common advocacy workflows, not named customer testimonials.

Data stewardship, stated plainly

Advocacy orgs have good reasons to care where their data lives and who can see it. Here is what we commit to — and what we will put in writing.

Your data is yours

Your queries, saved searches, alert rules, tags, and annotations belong to your organization. Export anytime as JSON or CSV. Cancel and take it with you.

Never sold to third parties

We will never sell, rent, or share tenant data with advertisers, data brokers, or any third party. Stated in our privacy policy and our data processing agreement.

Aggregated, anonymous analytics only

Internal usage metrics are aggregated and anonymized. We do not build personal profiles of the people using your tenant, and we do not track which member clicked which quote.

No tracking pixels on your public content

Public-facing pages you generate with CivicLens (digests, quote cards, embedded widgets) ship without third-party tracking pixels or analytics beacons by default.
