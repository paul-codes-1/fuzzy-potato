30-minute discovery call

Discovery Call Script

A conversational framework for CivicLens account executives. The goal of a discovery call is not to deliver a pitch. The goal is to find out whether the prospect has real pain, has a champion, and has budget authority. If those three things are present, the pilot sells itself. If any one of them is missing, no amount of pitching will save the deal.

1

Intro — 2 minutes

Orient, set the frame, get permission.

Objective

Confirm who is on the call and what they expected. Set a clear agenda. Reassure them this is discovery, not a demo ambush.

Sample language

"Thanks for making time. Before I dive in — I see I've got [names]. Is there anyone else we're expecting, or should we kick off?"
"Here's what I'd like to do with our 30 minutes: spend most of it learning about how your team handles meeting records today — what's working, what's painful, what you'd change. I'll save the last few minutes to tell you a little about CivicLens and see if a short pilot might make sense. Does that work for you?"
"And just to set expectations — this isn't a pitch call. If CivicLens isn't a fit I'll tell you that. I'd rather get to that answer in 30 minutes than waste six weeks of both our time."

Transition phrases

"Great — to start, I'd love to hear a bit about your role and what prompted you to take this call."

Pitfalls

— Don't launch into product talk. They've seen the website. They know what we do.

— Don't make small talk beyond 30 seconds. Respect the 30 minutes.

— Don't forget to confirm they have 30 minutes. A clerk with a council meeting at 6 PM may have less.

2

Context — 5 minutes

Five open questions. Shut up and take notes.

Objective

Understand who they are, why they took this call today, who else is involved, and what the political/budget context looks like. Do not advance until you have this.

The five open questions

1. "Tell me about your role — what does a typical week look like for you, and how does meeting content factor in?"
2. "What prompted you to take this call specifically today? Was there a moment, a project, a complaint, a budget cycle?"
3. "Who else in your organization would care about this? Clerk's office, IT, communications, mayor's office, council directly?"
4. "Is there a digital-transformation or AI initiative on the city side that this might fit into — or is it more of an operational pain-point you're trying to solve?"
5. "Walk me through where you are with meeting video and records today — are you on Granicus, Swagit, something else? How long has that been in place?"

Transition phrases

"That's really helpful context. Can I dig into the workflow side for a few minutes?"

Pitfalls

— Do not start solving yet. When they say "we use Granicus," do not launch into the vs-Granicus pitch.

— Do not ask more than five questions in this stage. If you blow through your clock here you'll skip pain, which is the whole point of the call.

— Do not interrupt an answer to move to the next question. Silence is fine. Let them fill it.

3

Current state — 5 minutes

Map the actual workflow. Names, hours, handoffs.

Objective

Get the prospect to describe, in operational detail, what happens before, during, and after a meeting. You are building a mental map of the swim lanes so you can identify where CivicLens fits.

Sample language

"Walk me through a typical council meeting from start to finish. Who preps the agenda? Who runs the video? Who writes the summary after?"
"And once the meeting ends — what does the next 24 hours look like? Who's doing what? When does the public or the press get anything?"
"How do you handle a FOIA or records request that references an old meeting? Who gets the request? What's their first move?"
"When a council member or the mayor asks 'what did we decide about X six months ago,' how does that get answered?"

What to write down

  • Named people and roles (use their titles later — "your clerk Denise")
  • Estimated hours per meeting on summaries, research, FOIA
  • Tools in the stack: Granicus, Legistar, Novus, Swagit, CivicPlus, SharePoint, Google Drive
  • Whether a transcript exists today at all
  • Any moment where they visibly sigh — that is the pain you will anchor on next

Transition phrases

"That's really clear. So if I'm hearing right, [play back workflow]. I want to go a little deeper on the parts that sound the most time-consuming — can I ask a few follow-ups?"

Pitfalls

— Don't judge their current stack. If they use Novus, don't say "oh, that's limited." Say "got it, tell me more about how it handles minutes."

— Don't fill gaps with assumptions. If they don't know how a step works, write "unknown" and circle back.

4

Pain — 8 minutes (the most important stage)

Quantify the pain. Get emotion on the record. Find the cost of inaction.

Objective

Move the prospect from "this could be nice" to "this is actually costing us something I can quantify." Get the pain into their own words so you can quote it back in the proposal.

Probing questions

"What's the worst version of this? The week where it all went wrong — tell me that story."
"If the clerk retired tomorrow, what breaks? Who would know how to find things?"
"How many hours a week would you estimate go into meeting-related research and summary work across your team?"
"Has council or the mayor complained about search, transparency, or response times? Has a journalist written anything?"
"When was your last FOIA request and how long did it take? Were you close to the deadline?"
"If this stayed exactly as it is for the next 12 months, what's the downside? And is there an upside to fixing it now versus next year?"

The silent move

When they describe a pain, do not immediately offer a solution. Instead: pause, write it down, and say one of:

"That sounds exhausting. How often does that happen?"
"And when that happens — how does it feel for the team?"
"Got it. How would you measure what that costs you today?"

Transition phrases

"Okay — I think I've got a pretty clear picture of where it hurts. Can I describe what a better version might look like, and you can tell me if that's actually valuable or if I'm off?"

Pitfalls

— Do not sell yet. Pain stage is for listening, not pitching.

— Do not make the clerk feel criticized. Frame pain as "the system is broken," not "you are behind."

— Do not accept "it's fine" as an answer. If they are on this call, something prompted it. Ask what.

— Do not let the conversation be entirely rational. Emotional pain (frustration, embarrassment, burnout) drives deals more than ROI math.

5

Future state — 5 minutes

Paint the vision in their words, then tie CivicLens to it briefly.

Objective

Reflect the pain back and describe what "solved" looks like, using specific examples from their own workflow. Then — and only then — briefly describe how CivicLens fits. Do not do a full demo in this stage. Save it for a second call.

Sample language

"So imagine this. A resident emails your clerk asking what council decided about short-term rentals last spring. Today that's a two-hour dig. In the future state, your clerk types the question into a search bar and gets a cited answer in five seconds — with a link that jumps straight to the video moment. Is that valuable to [named clerk]?"
"Or imagine the morning after a council meeting. Instead of your clerk writing a summary from scratch, there's already a draft waiting in her inbox with the votes, the financial items, and the public comment section broken out. She edits and publishes in 15 minutes. Is that the right picture?"
"That's essentially what CivicLens does. We sit on top of your existing Granicus [or whatever they use] video archive and turn the content into structured, searchable, queryable intelligence. No migration, no change to your clerk's current tools on day one."

Transition phrases

"Is that the right direction, or is there a piece of the picture I'm missing?"
"Assuming this is interesting — can we talk about what a short pilot would look like for your team?"

Pitfalls

— Do not turn this into a feature tour. You can say "we also do highlights, email digests, vote tracking" but do not demo all of them.

— Do not use generic vision language. Use their clerk's name, their council's name, their city's last real issue. "Short-term rentals" beats "policy items."

— Do not promise what you cannot ship. If they ask "can you integrate with our homegrown permitting system," say "probably, let's take that offline."

6

Next steps — 5 minutes

Never leave without a concrete commitment on both sides.

Objective

Lock in a specific next step with a date on the calendar. "We'll circle back" is a failed call. "Let's book a 30-minute demo with your clerk and IT director next Tuesday at 2 PM" is a successful one.

The commitment ladder

Pick the highest rung they'll agree to, not the lowest. Start high and ratchet down only if they resist.

High: "Let's set up a 30-minute working session with your clerk and your IT director next week. I'll demo against your actual archive so it's real to them."
Middle: "Let me send a short proposal for a 90-day pilot, scoped to your council archive, and schedule a follow-up to walk through it together."
Lower: "Would it be useful if I sent our procurement kit and the security whitepaper so your IT team can pre-review before we meet again?"
Lowest: "Can I send you a short recap email and three links — our product tour, the vs-Granicus comparison, and a case study from [similar city] — and book a 20-minute follow-up two weeks from now?"

What to always ask before hanging up

  • "Who else inside the city needs to hear about this? Can I be on the next conversation with them?"
  • "What's the budget or procurement path if we did move forward? Is it a purchase order, a cooperative contract, a grant?"
  • "Is there anything I can send that would help you make the case internally?"
  • "Is there any reason you can think of, right now, that this wouldn't work for you?"

Sample closing language

"Appreciate you walking me through all of that. Honestly — this sounds like a strong fit. Here's what I'd love to do next: [specific ask]. Does that work?"
"I'll send a recap by end of day today with the links I mentioned and a calendar invite for our next meeting. If anything changes on your end, just reply to that email."

Pitfalls

— Do not end the call with "I'll send you some materials." That is a death sentence. Always put a date on the calendar.

— Do not propose a pilot without knowing who signs the PO. Find the budget owner before you price anything.

— Do not be afraid to disqualify. If this is not a fit, say so: "Honestly, based on what you've described, I think you'd be over-buying with us today. Let's stay in touch in six months." Burned prospects will remember and come back.

After the call (within 2 hours)

  • → Log call notes in CRM: pain points verbatim, stack, budget owner, next step with date.
  • → Send recap email with three links (product tour, relevant comparison page, case study).
  • → Send calendar invite for the committed next step.
  • → Flag any claim you were unsure about (competitor feature, pricing, compliance) and chase it down before the next call.
  • → If you heard an objection you couldn't answer, add it to the #sales-objections channel for team review.
